Browsers support automatic hyphenation using language-specific dictionaries.
Unfortunately, the current standards do not allow browser engines to discriminate between different types of hyphenation opportunities. Compound word boundaries are treated the same as syllable breaks.

Many public sector sites are required to use [plain language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_language) to ensure readers can easily find, understand and use information. The requirement includes the structure and visual design of the text.

**As the current spec treats all line-break opportunities equally, automatic hyphenation can slow down reading speed and hinder comprehension, conflicting with these plain language requirements.**

Breaking words between syllables can make it significantly harder for readers to recognize the individual parts of a word, forcing the reader to re-scan it.

In compound-heavy languages, it is still necessary to allow line breaks in words. Otherwise they would overflow, or cause large empty gaps when they are forced entirely to a new line. In these situations, breaking the words at the compound joins is less likely to cause issues with reading speed and comprehension.

Currently, the only way to achieve this is to manually insert soft hyphens to ensure words break at the desired locations. This is time-consuming and tedious, and is often omitted by authors.

### Proposed solution:

Option 1:
Allow authors to define the type of hyphenation opportunities that can be taken through a property such as `hyphenate-mode: compound-only`

Option 2:
Allow authors to provide a custom hyphenation dictionary. An [early version of `css-text`](https://www.w3.org/TR/2011/WD-css3-text-20110412/#hyphenation-resource) included custom hyphenation resources, but this was dropped in later revisions.

## @Crissov — 2026-10-22

Technically, soft breaks inserted manually in markup create [_soft wrap opportunities_](https://drafts.csswg.org/css-text-4/#text-wrapping), not [_hyphenation opportunities_](https://drafts.csswg.org/css-text-4/#hyphenation). Languages that use a hyphen to combine simple words into complex compounds (_mark-up_) therefore could rely on word wrapping, i.e. `text-wrap-mode: wrap`. The current German orthography supports hyphen-compounding for edge cases, but does not mandate it in general; it’s employed more frequently in texts that are written in “simplified language”. 
In other languages, the distinction between compounds (lexeme + joiner? + lexeme) and affixed words (prefix\* + lex\[infix?\]eme + postfix\*) is not simple – or not meaningful. 

It is often assumed that a hyphen creates a preferred or an exclusive word-break opportunity, which may even override otherwise applicable restrictions, but at least in German it may be only a last-resort break point, e.g. if it is preceded or followed by just one or two letters (or digits), as in _H-Milch, x-fach, 6-Jährige, Kung-fu_. Although this can be handled by `hyphenate-limit-chars`, it may be surprising that, for instance, _U-Bahnlinie_ should be split between _n_ and _l_.

Some orthographies may also prefer hyphenation opportunities before word-internal capital letters (as in _MacDonald_, _kiSuaheli_), whereas Apple (_iPhone_) and German (_LeserIn_) would usually discourage those (and they operate outside traditional rules), although they have also been used as an alternative to hyphens in compounds (_BinnenMajuskel_). 

Some languages – again including German, especially `de-Latf` – have (had) alternative hyphenation rulesets, where one prioritizes phonographical breaks and the other prefers semantic or etymological breaks, e.g. _mag-net_ vs. _ma-gnet_. 
Some traditional rules may only make sense when certain typographic conventions are followed, e.g. ligatures or long vs. round s.

Many alphabetic orthographies allow certain other non-letter characters within words (i.e. between letters, or even after or before them), e.g. the apostrophe `’` in English contractions or the dot `.` in abbreviations, which would otherwise be considered punctuation marks. This includes “gender gap” markers in German, e.g. _Leser\_in_, _Leser:in_ or _Leser\*in_ (besides classic _Leser(in)_ and _Leser/-in_). Authors may accept these to be treated as wrapping opportunities, but whether a hyphen should be dyed remains as unclear as which line that marker (`_:*`) should go in.

---

For most of these stylistic choices, authors would likely prefer any hyphenation over non, but this may differ for compounds in “easy language” texts.

It is a bit unclear to me whether finer control should be handled as part of the `hyphen…` properties (e.g. the proposed `hyphenate-mode`) or reuse the [`line-break`](https://drafts.csswg.org/css-text-4/#line-break-property) values currently only specified explicitly for Chinese and Japanese text. 
However, I think allowing (possibly unhyphenated) breaks before word-internal majuscules or punctuation marks should be handled by the latter (e.g. okay for `loose`, but not for `strict`), the rest by the former. 

~~~~ ebnf
hyphens: none | <'hyphenation-mode'> | auto
hyphenation-mode: _manual_
               || structure/morphologic/morpheme 
               || meaning/semantic/lexeme 
               || sound/phonologic/syllable 
               || look/typographic 

line-break: _auto_ | loose | normal | strict | anywhere
~~~~
