What should happen to content clamped by `line-clamp` in the accessibility tree?

Just like content made invisible to sighted users by `visibility: hidden`, I think that content made invisible to sighted users using `line-clamp` should also be hidden from screen-reader users.

<!-- comment id=3340695474 author=SebastianZ created=2025-09-26T22:33:21Z url=https://github.com/w3c/csswg-drafts/issues/12859#issuecomment-3340695474 -->
## @SebastianZ — 2025-09-26

The same question probably also applies to `overflow`, maybe in combination with `text-overflow`.

> I think that content made invisible to sighted users using `line-clamp` should also be hidden from screen-reader users.

I am not sure about that. Line clamping is often used to harmonize layout, e.g. when you have several article teasers on a page or you have a list/table with titles in it. In those cases authors often limit the number of lines or the width of the column to avoid "fringing", i.e. visually cluttering the page. Though often that effect is only meant to be visual and authors sometimes display the full text in a tooltip.
In such cases, I'd expect a screen reader to read the full text.

On the other hand, if an author clamps a full text that would otherwise span many lines to just two or three, it might make sense to also hide that from screen readers. The question then is, what they are supposed to output in those cases. Just stopping in the middle of a sentence or even a word seems pretty bad for UX.

Sebastian

## @frivoal — 2025-10-06

> The same question probably also applies to overflow

I'm not convinced. `overflow: scroll` hides content, but also gives you a way to access it. `line-clamp` just hides (and if a way to access the hidden content is provided by the author, it's by removing `line-clamp`).

If the full text is displayed somehow (including in a tooltip), then I'd think about it differently, but as specified, that is not the case, the remainder of the content is just not visible.

In general, I think screen readers, as their names indicates, are intended to give an audio rendition of what's on screen, not an independent audio rendition of the source document regardless of how it's styled.

If something is invisible yet read aloud, I believe that can be confusing, for instance in cases where a (partially) sighted user is accessing the page with a screen reader, and cannot easily reconcile what they're hearing with what they're seeing, or in case where a blind user discusses the content of the page with a sighted user, and they have a different understanding of what's there.

> Just stopping in the middle of a sentence or even a word seems pretty bad for UX.

That is exactly the UX that a sighted user is getting, though. As long as the ellipsis or some audio equivalent is rendered, I'm not certain that's an issue.

That said, I am not personally a screen reader user, nor have I don't user research on this specific question, so my guesses as to what's best are just that: guesses.

## @cookiecrook — 2026-03-13

Yes. 

Let’s say we have the following sentence:

“Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, consectetur adipiscing elit.”

And in the default font size the line clamp renders:

> Lorem ipsum dolor sit 
> amet, consectetur…

And in the case of a low-vision, sighted screenreader user (who uses a combination of Larger Text, VoiceOver, Zoom, and HoverText) the font might be extra large, rendering only the following:

> Lorem 
> ipsum…

But a different sighted user with better than average vision (or a visually impaired person with high clarity but low peripheral vision) could also set their font size to xx-small, and the whole sentence would be rendered:

> Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet, 
> consectetur adipiscing elit. 

In the above three possible renderings of this single hypothetical scenario, the low vision screen reader user should not be penalized for displaying large text, especially if the large text benefits their remaining usable vision. Clamping the Accessibility API label value to the visibly rendered text would prevent their screen reader from voicing the entire text, and it would prevent tools like HoverText from being able to re-render the text in a large tooltip-like view, the same text that their sighted colleagues can read visually.

Moving on from the low vision screen reader user example, many fully blind screen reader users don’t always know (nor should they have to care) whether their browser window is rendered large or small. Changing what is exposed to the accessibility tree this way could leave them with unpredictable results. One day being able to access content at the end of the line clamp, and the next day finding that content missing due to it being rendered in a slightly smaller window.

## @jcsteh — 2026-06-26

As is often the case, the correct behaviour here comes down to the author's intent. If the author ultimately intends all of the text to be perceivable but the text can't fit purely for reasons of visual layout, then I'd argue the text should be exposed to accessibility APIs. There are a lot of truncation cases like this. The visual parallel might be that the text is exposed with a tooltip, that you can zoom to expose more of the text, etc. Personally, I lean more in this direction in this case.

On the other hand, if the author does not intend all of the text to be exposed - i.e. this is not purely a presentational restriction, but is rather a fundamental intention of the UI - then it shouldn't be exposed to accessibility APIs.

This is another situation where I think we hit a conflict of principles. CSS is generally thought of as being specific to visual styling, so the general sense is that CSS properties are less about intent and more about visual layout. Thus, there's a tendency to define CSS properties purely with respect to their visual impact, covering as many use cases as possible, and therefore the answer is "we don't know what the author intends". But that conflicts somewhat with accessibility, where the intent/use case is fundamental to choosing the correct behaviour. Ideally, I think the property (or properties) need to make it clear what the intent is, rather than this being a guessing game on the part of authors, accessibility APIs, assistive tech or users.

<!-- comment id=4806447322 author=SebastianZ created=2026-06-26T04:48:49Z url=https://github.com/w3c/csswg-drafts/issues/12859#issuecomment-4806447322 -->
## @SebastianZ — 2026-06-26

Very well expressed, @jcsteh! Two points I'd add to this:

If it's the authors intent to _not_ expose all of the text - neither visually nor aurally - then the cropping should already happen in the DOM. If the cropping is meant to only be presentational, then I'd say this should be done in CSS, visually _and_ aurally.

And just to emphasize this again, CSS is not just about _visual_ rendering of contents but any form of presentation, hence the [CSS Speech spec](https://drafts.csswg.org/css-speech-1/), [CSS Spatial Nav](https://drafts.csswg.org/css-spatial-nav-1/), etc. So again, authors should get at least _some_ control over what gets exposed to AT to be able to express their intent, but it is also important to provide a good default (like it's always the case for any CSS feature we introduce).

Sebastian

## @johannesodland — 2026-06-26

A fairly common design requirement we get is to show a few rows of a table, a few items of a list or a few lines of text, and allow the user to click a button to expand and see the rest.

The intent here is to show the most important parts, or tease a much longer content, and allow the most interested users to expand for a deep dive, while allowing "regular" users to skip past. This is often used with result tables, or editorial deep dives like "This is the method we used to find...". 

With tables and lists, this is easy to implement by hiding elements with `display:none`, but with free form text, it is more complicated as there are no "line elements" to hide. `line-clamp` will help in that regards.

Since the intent is to hide the remaining content from all users, it should be possible to hide it from AT as well. Giving them a different experience would be confusing. Additionally, we could end up trapping them inside a long deep dive that was intended to be hidden.

If the clamped content is exposed to AT by default, we would need a new property that allows authors to hide it if that was the intention.
